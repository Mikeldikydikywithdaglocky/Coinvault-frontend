Account safety

Spot crypto scams before you act.

A convincing message is not proof of a trustworthy sender. Use these five checks before you click, share account details or send money.

5 practical checks·Free educational guide

01

Pause when a message creates pressure.

Threats, urgent deadlines and promises of easy profit are reasons to stop and check. A countdown, familiar logo or polished dashboard does not prove that an offer is genuine.

  • Do not act on an unexpected demand to move money to a supposedly safe wallet.
  • Be cautious of guaranteed returns, unsolicited investment advice and people asking you to keep an investment secret.
Read the FTC's crypto-scam guidance
02

Check the sender through a separate channel.

Open a website you already know or use the provider's official app. Contact the organisation using details you find independently, not a phone number or link from the suspicious message.

Check the complete website address and sender email. A copied logo or a secure-connection padlock is not proof that the person behind a website can be trusted.

Example message

“Your account closes in 10 minutes. Send your verification code to our agent to keep your wallet active.”
  • An urgent threat is pushing you to act quickly.
  • An agent is asking for a secret sign-in code.
  • The safe next step is independent verification, not replying.
Read the FTC's phishing guide
03

Keep account and wallet secrets private.

Do not send anyone your password, one-time verification code, recovery phrase or private key. A wallet recovery phrase can give another person control of the wallet.

  • Use a unique password for each account and a reputable password manager.
  • Enable multifactor authentication wherever your email or wallet provider offers it.
  • Keep your device and browser updated. Do not install remote-access software at the request of an unsolicited support agent.
CISA's online-safety checklist (PDF)
04

Question requests for more money.

Before approving any transfer, independently confirm who receives it, the amount and the purpose. Crypto transfers are usually irreversible. An animation, displayed balance or transaction screenshot is not proof that funds are available or that a payment has settled.

Be cautious of new fees to unlock funds.

Demands for an additional payment, tax or purchased “unlock key” before you can access supposed investment earnings are a serious warning sign. Stop sending money and verify the platform independently. Disclosed, genuine transaction fees are not the same as invented charges to release an investment.

Do not pay someone who promises to recover lost crypto for an upfront fee. Recovery promises can be another scam.

Read the FBI's investment-fraud warnings
05

Act promptly if something seems wrong.

  1. Stop contact and further payments. Do not send another payment to release or recover the first one.
  2. Secure affected accounts. From a trusted device, change exposed passwords and contact your provider if codes or wallet secrets were shared.
  3. Keep evidence. Save messages, receipts, website addresses and transaction references. Do not include passwords or recovery phrases in reports.
  4. Contact the payment provider. Tell your bank, mobile-money network or exchange what happened as soon as possible. A refund or reversal is not guaranteed.
  5. Report locally. Contact the appropriate police or fraud authority in your country. US users can also report through ReportFraud.ftc.gov or IC3.gov.

A quick reality check

Does a familiar logo make a message trustworthy?

No. A logo, sender name or professional-looking page can be copied. Verify through a contact method you already trust.

Should I send my email code to a support agent?

No. Keep verification codes private. Only enter one into the genuine service when you initiated the relevant sign-in or account action.

Does a successful small withdrawal prove an investment is safe?

No. Fraudulent platforms may allow a small withdrawal to build confidence before asking for larger deposits. Verify the company independently rather than relying on a test payment.
